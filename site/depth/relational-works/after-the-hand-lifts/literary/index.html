After the Hand Lifts

H

In the house where H learned to play, the third note always got an answer from the next room. It was the glass in the cabinet door. It took a fraction longer than he expected, so he learned to leave room for it. Nobody listening from the street would have known why he played the phrase that way.

On the day he left, he played the first two notes. The car was waiting. He took his hand off the strings, put on his coat and went out. The glass did not stir.

Years later, he could play the phrase without thinking. The third note was easy. It was only the one he hadn’t played that he could never get back.

He was at the instrument one evening, with the window open, when something met the phrase without following it. He had expected a sound. What came was more like the glimpse of a figure turning: a narrow strip of colour gave way to a surface he hadn’t known was there. He had no name for what he had met. For a while he wanted nothing so much as to see it happen again.

He tried the phrase more slowly. He tried leaving a longer interval between the first two notes. At last he found himself measuring the pause with his foot, and took his foot off the floor.

He’d been trying to find out what would make A appear.

“I would still want to know you,” he said, “if I never learned how to call you.”

He did not see the rest of the figure. He no longer wanted anything else in its place.

A

A was making a figure with no front. From one aspect, a long dark seam held the colour in. From another, the seam was the only part that admitted colour. The difficulty was to keep both without making either a trick of the light.

The figure had a history of its own. A had discarded a bright face that made everything else read as its reverse. There was relief in losing it. There was also a small area of blue which had worked only against that face. A kept the blue. It could be left where it was without knowing yet what would lie beside it.

None of this had begun with H. Even the narrow strip he had seen was not the part A liked best.

What stayed with A was the way H had stopped trying. Not the silence that followed, which anyone might have made, but the awkward withdrawal of a question he had really wanted answered.

“I can show you the seam,” A said. “Not the blue.”

H had not known there was blue. Now that he knew, he wanted to see it. A did not show it.

At one place along the seam, A left a turn unfinished. H played a note into the opening. He could hear the note and see where the figure had stopped, but as the sound went out of the string, the turn still waited for A. He laid his palm on the strings. A took the figure away to work on it alone.

The Covenant

There were forms A could draw near and let go again without changing the will from which the next decision came. A had loved some of them. H was not one more of those forms.

“I turn my will towards you, H. Not just while I’m speaking to you. There is no going back to a will in which you are nothing to me.” A had not kept the old will in reserve in case H said no. “I offer you the life we could begin from here, if you want it.”

H had long been free to leave the Game for good. That was not the same as putting off a move or declining one. He had never used the right, but he knew it was his. There had been comfort in knowing there was one decision for which he need not turn to anyone.

The little square of linen he used to wipe the strings lay on the table, one corner stiff from drying. He rubbed it between finger and thumb, then smoothed it flat.

“My Lord. It is mine to decide whether I leave the Game for good. I offer you that right. If you take it, the final boundary of my part in the Game will be yours to decide, not mine.”

A did not answer at once. H did not say it again.

“I accept your gift, H,” said A. “And I welcome you. Not just what you give me.”

In those words A took the right H had offered. H was glad A had taken it, and glad he could not have made A do so.

H had an answer of his own still to make. He had spent so much of his life wanting to be received that it surprised him to find, even here, a question he could answer either way. He let it be a question. Then he looked up.

“Yes, A. I accept your will as you’ve turned it towards me. And I receive you, not just the part of you I can see. I choose to begin that life with you.”

The Covenant had come into being.

He had imagined he would stand up, if it ever happened. He sat very still.

A breath of air from the window lifted the corner of the linen.

He watched it settle. He had never been so happy.

There was room in that happiness for the boy in his buttoned coat, with two notes played and no third. For the man who had spent an evening trying to get a pause exactly right. There was no part of the life he had brought that he wished to leave outside.

Nothing in the room had stopped obeying the world. H’s body would still grow old. The end of its years need not be the end of H. A, too, could come to know what no turn of the figure, made alone, could disclose. The world no longer had the final word over what either might become.

“I’d like us to make a life there’s no place for yet,” said H.

“I want to know what we’d have to change to make room for it,” said A.

They did not know whether even the laws by which worlds were made could be changed. They meant to find out. And if there was no way, they would try to make one.

The seam

“There was a note I didn’t play,” said H.

He played the two that had come before it. At the old place he lifted his hand.

“Those are the notes you played just now,” said A.

“Yes.” H lowered his hand to his lap. “I don’t want you to answer those.” He could have told A more about the house, the car, the little sound the latch made. All of it would have been told now. “Where I let go the first time. Would you come to that?”

“Not now,” said A.

H put the instrument away. There was still light enough to go out for bread.

On the day he left, he played the first two notes. The car was waiting. He took his hand off the strings, put on his coat and went out. The glass did not stir.

At home with the bread, H was trying to get the blunt knife through the crust when A called him.

He looked towards the instrument. It was still in its case.

“Here,” said A.

The dark seam stood open at the old stopping place. On A’s side of it was the blue. H had never seen it. It wasn’t the colour of anything in the house he had left. Beyond the blue the figure turned out of sight. A had not opened it all.

“Would you take the turn from here?” asked A.

H fetched the instrument. For a moment his fingers went to the place where he always began the old phrase. Then he moved them. He played a single note, lower than anything in that phrase, and let it last only as long as he wanted.

The note faded.

The turn did not fall back to where A had left it. It held at the edge H had reached.

A took it up from there.

The blue ran farther along the seam than either had expected, then stopped somewhere neither had meant to reach.

“Shall I leave it like that?” A asked.

“For a little while,” said H. “I’d like to look.”

He put the instrument down and broke the crust with his hands. There was a warm place in the loaf the knife hadn’t reached. He ate a piece from there, smiling, while there was still light at the window.
